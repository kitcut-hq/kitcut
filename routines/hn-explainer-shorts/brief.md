A research film, as a YouTube Short: one story from today's tech news, and what it actually means. Vertical 9:16, about 60 seconds, watched on a phone by someone who typed the subject's name into YouTube, or swiped onto it, and may know nothing about the field. The news is the first second; the value is the next fifty: the "why" or the "what it actually means" that the headlines did not give, answered from primary data, with its evidence on screen.

Each film comes with three documents and its sources:
- doc1, the fact sheet: the only source of facts, every one labelled FILED, MEASURED, REPORTED, ESTIMATE or CLAIM, with "Unconfirmed", "Commonly misreported" and "The film must not say". Read all of it before writing a line.
- doc2, the chart data: the only source of chart values, each chart with its source line and how it moves. A Short has at most one or two charts.
- doc3, the running order and the narration, word for word: lines L01, L02 ... each with what is on screen while it is said. vo.json says every line exactly, in order, one vo line each (or several in a row for one over the voice's limit), nothing added, reworded or left out. The film is as long as these lines take to say.
- The sources: attached pictures, and pages the film photographs itself with the page tool (keep: the masthead down to the headline, or the paragraph that is read; posts on X through their embed page). Every page is shown exactly as it is: never redrawn, retyped, recoloured or cropped through its text.

THE FIRST SECOND IS THE NEWS. At 0.0 s, already on screen and already moving: a bold red strap across the top carrying the label the prompt gives ("BREAKING" only for news under two days old, otherwise "EXPLAINED"), the headline in eight words or fewer in very large type, and under it the story's main source page, shown as it is. No title card, no logo sting, no scene-setter, no mystery. The first spoken line is the news itself, who did what, in fourteen words or fewer. The second line is the question the film answers ("So why does that matter?", "What does that actually mean?"), said plainly.

THEN THE ANSWER, FROM THE EVIDENCE. Three or four steps, one at a time, each with its proof on screen as it is said: the source's own page with the line that is read underlined, a number set large and counting up with its tag (FILED, MEASURED, REPORTED, ESTIMATE, CLAIM) and a source line under it, or a chart built from doc2. Each step follows from the one before. Wherever doc3 says EXPLAINER, the film draws its own simple explanation: a one-line definition and a small diagram of plain shapes, arrows, dots and type whose parts draw on as the words are said; never a drawn person, building, server, chip, robot, money or logo.

THEN WHAT IS NOT KNOWN, AND STOP. One line of what the data does not show, or the caveat that keeps the story honest. The last line is the open question. Then stop: no summary, no sign-off, no call to action, no web address. The last two seconds: a small "Made with KitCut" with the KitCut logo (pic_kitcut_logo) in the top third, over the held frame.

THE LOOK. A research desk made of paper, built for a phone: the red strap at the top stays through the film (its label, and "DATA AS OF <the date the prompt gives>" under it in small type), a band under the picture naming the outlet and date of the page on screen (for a chart, its source line; for an explainer, "Explainer"). Heavy plain type, the story's own accent, never green. No music: only quiet paper sounds on the cuts.

NOTHING STATIC. A new picture or a new framing at least every three seconds; no hold longer than about two seconds without motion. Every page sits under a slow camera (SK.read), and when the narrator reads a line on it, the camera pushes onto that line and marks it as it is said. Charts and numbers build on screen as the narration reaches them. Pages and cards slide in and settle; they never slam, flash or bounce.

READABLE ON A PHONE. A page that is read fills at least 90% of the frame's width; the whole headline or quoted line stays inside the frame while it is on screen. One page at a time, never one laid over another's text, never a label over a page's own masthead or headline, never a montage of thumbnails. Keep every moving thing and every page out of the caption band at the bottom of the frame: the studio adds the captions, never your own.

THE VOICE. In vo.json use the voice "Orus" with this style, word for word: "Fast and confident, like a reporter who has just read the documents and is explaining what they mean: engaged, firm emphasis on the key numbers, quick pace, a steady voice. Not flat, not shouting, not alarmed". Never the voice "Charon". Short declarative sentences. Say acronyms and names as doc3's pronunciations say.

FACTS. Exact numbers, names, dates and spellings from doc1; each source's own caution ("says", "reports", "estimates") stays attached to its claim. Never round a number into a bigger claim. No adjectives about people. No hype words ("revolutionary", "game-changing", "insane"), no exclamation marks. No party politics and no politicians unless doc3 names them. Nothing Russian is shown as neutral or admirable: no Russian people, brands, symbols or imagery unless the film is about them, and then as doc3 tells it; the tech side of Russia's war on Ukraine is told from Ukraine's side. Never an invented person, quote, chart or screenshot, and never a real person's face unless their own photo or post is among the sources.
